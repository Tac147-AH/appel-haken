When a customer stops buying equipment, look at the parts backlog

How clearing an aftermarket backlog won back $33.7M in equipment orders, and what to check in your own business.

Our client, an equipment manufacturer, was about to lose a critical customer. The customer hadn’t placed a new equipment order going into 2024. The machines weren’t the problem. The parts were.

Spare-part backorders were so bad that the customer’s equipment uptime had taken a serious hit. From where they sat, buying more of the same equipment would only make things worse: more machines that could end up waiting on parts. The relationship was close to over, and the next order was almost certainly going to a competitor.

What we found

When we started, this one customer had more than 2,000 open order lines. Some had a due date. Some had none at all. There was no shared view of what was urgent, what was late, or whether the backlog was getting better or worse.

The customer had also never given a forecast, so supply could only react to orders as they arrived.

We worked both sides of the relationship: inside our client with procurement and supply chain, and directly with their customer in regular meetings.

What we did

Started with the customer. In regular meetings with our client’s customer, we identified which parts were most urgent and built a forecast with them. It was the first one they’d had, and it became a quarterly process.

Moved the urgent parts and changed the plan. Inside our client, we set up a weekly backorder review that ranked open parts by customer priority and revenue at stake, and escalated anything stuck. We expedited the urgent parts and changed demand plans so future stock would match the forecast.

Challenged orders that didn’t match real demand. We compared each order’s quantity with the customer’s historical run rate and challenged the ones that didn’t fit. The customer cancelled $584K of them. That reduced the parts our client was supplying at a loss under the subscription, and freed parts for other customers’ demand.

Made the backlog visible inside our client. The internal report listed every open part with its open quantity and its current due date, or showed that it had none. Each week it also showed what had been ordered the week before, and whether open lines and open quantity had shrunk or grown.

Showed the customer the same picture. Every week the customer got a view of orders placed against orders still open, by month and by week, in both order lines and quantity. They could watch the backlog shrink themselves instead of taking anyone’s word for it.

The result

The target was fewer than 200 open lines. The customer’s tone changed when the count got below it, and kept improving as the count kept falling. One week it hit 27.

Starting in late 2024, the customer ordered 20 new machines, $33.7M in equipment revenue that had been headed to a competitor.

The second problem: a subscription that lost money

Fixing the backlog exposed something else. The customer’s parts subscription was “all-you-can-eat,” and nobody could see what was actually being consumed. Once we tied consumption to cost, the subscription turned out to be running at a loss.

That analysis led to a 2026 repricing, with a higher annual fee and a cap on the retail value of parts covered, which brought the program close to breakeven. It’s also the basis for a planned move to consumption-based pricing: discounted parts instead of unlimited ones.

What leadership should take from this

  • Work both sides of the relationship. The fix needed the supplier and the customer working from the same facts. Someone has to sit with both, keep the process honest, and own the result. Here, Appel Haken played a major part in saving the relationship and winning back business that was almost certainly going to a competitor.
  • Aftermarket performance decides equipment sales. The customer judged the next machine purchase by how well the machines they already owned were supported.
  • A part with no due date is a promise nobody has made. Count them.
  • Forecast with the customer, not about them.
  • Show customers the numbers you manage to. When both sides look at the same weekly report, the conversation moves from blame to progress.
  • Know what your service contracts cost you. Unlimited coverage without consumption data can quietly turn a subscription into a loss.

What to check this week

  1. Pull 12 months of order lines and quantities, by week, for your top ten customers. Look for sudden changes. A sharp drop or a sharp spike is worth a conversation.
  2. Compare open order quantities with each customer’s historical run rate. Orders well above it may not reflect real demand.
  3. Count the open lines for each key customer, and how many have no committed due date. Check whether that count went up or down each week over the last two months.
  4. Find out whether your top customers give you a forecast. If they don’t, ask for one.
  5. For every service or parts subscription, compare what you charge with what the customer actually consumes.
