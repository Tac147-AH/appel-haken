Your contract manufacturers are paying different prices for the same part

How one consolidated view of price paid by part number found $2.53M of opportunity in $24.6M of spend, and a 30-minute test to run on your own.

Our client, an equipment manufacturer, built its products through four contract manufacturers. Each one bought components on our client’s behalf and passed the cost through. Together that was about $24.6M a year, spread across 8,528 components and 70 bills of material.

Nobody could answer a simple question: are we paying the same price for the same part everywhere?

Why the answer was hidden

The data existed. Every supplier sent cost data regularly. It just couldn’t be compared:

  • Every supplier used its own format. Four files, four layouts.
  • Part numbers didn’t match. Each supplier had its own naming for the same component.
  • Units of measure varied from supplier to supplier.
  • Make versus buy differed by supplier. One supplier made a sheet metal part in house. Another had to buy the same part from someone else.

With the data in that state, each supplier’s pricing could only be reviewed on its own terms. The comparison that creates leverage never happened.

What we did

We wrote scripts that standardize each supplier’s cost file and put them into one usable form. That produced a single view of total spend, suppliers, and price paid by part number. For every common part, our client could see what each supplier was paying and where the gaps were.

Now that the scripts know every supplier’s format, each new cost file runs straight through. The view stays current without anyone rebuilding a spreadsheet.

What it found

$2.53M of opportunity across the $24.6M, a little over 10% of spend.

The next step was straightforward: take the parts where one supplier was paying more, and renegotiate those costs down to the lowest price already being paid elsewhere. That work is under way.

What leadership should take from this

  • When a contract manufacturer buys for you, you pay for its purchasing performance. Its prices become your costs, whether or not you see them.
  • Your best benchmark is a price you already pay. It isn’t a market estimate or a consultant’s number, so it’s hard to argue with.
  • Messy data hides leverage. It doesn’t need new software to fix. It needs someone who understands the parts and can standardize the data.
  • Make versus buy inside your suppliers affects your costs. Know which parts each one makes and which it buys.

A 30-minute test you can run this week

  1. Pick a handful of common parts that more than one supplier buys on your behalf.
  2. Get the current price each supplier pays for each part.
  3. Put them in one table, in the same unit of measure.
  4. Look for inconsistencies. Where prices differ, multiply the gap by annual volume to see what it’s worth.

If the gaps are real on a handful of parts, they’re almost certainly larger across the full bill of material.
